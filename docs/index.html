How this works

This tool implements the odor-classification models from Mayhew et al., “Transport features predict if a molecule is odorous,” PNAS 2022. The central idea: a molecule smells only if it can physically complete the journey to an olfactory receptor. That journey is governed by simple transport properties — volatility (vapor pressure / boiling point) and hydrophobicity (logP). Molecules that are too heavy or too polar never reach the receptors and are odorless.

Two models from the paper are used here:

  • Rule of three — a molecule with molecular weight between 30 and 300 Da and fewer than four heteroatoms is generally odorous. Works for any structure and is computed entirely in your browser. In the paper, 96% of molecules meeting these criteria are odorous.
  • Transport boundary model — logistic-regression boundaries in vapor pressure / logP space (AUROC ≈ 0.88–0.98). Requires a vapor-pressure value.

When you enter one of the 1,924 molecules from the study's curated dataset, the tool also shows the actual gradient-boosted transport-ML probability reported in the paper. Vapor pressure and logP for other molecules come from the EPA CompTox and EPI Suite databases: a measured value when either has one, otherwise the OPERA prediction, and otherwise EPI Suite's MPBPVP (vapor pressure) or KOWWIN (logP) estimate. The boundaries were fit on Moriguchi logP, so applying them to other octanol–water logP scales is an approximation.

Does it have an odor? The panel weighs every line of evidence the site has and leads with the strongest one available: what people report (the published odor atlases and the paper's labels) › the transport-ML model › the transport boundaries › the rule of three. Weaker lines that disagree are shown, not hidden — musk ketone, for instance, fails the rule of three on heteroatom count, yet every atlas that lists it describes a musky odor. Structure parsing, 2-D depiction, molecular weight, logP and heteroatom counts are computed client-side with RDKit (WebAssembly). Chemical-name lookup uses the public PubChem API.

Inhalation reference

For the ~13,000 molecules in the site's tables, the tool also reports how many sniffs of the neat (undiluted) headspace would together deliver the molecule's daily Threshold of Toxicological Concern (TTC). The TTC is assigned with Toxtree 3.1: a mutagenicity structural alert (ISS in-vitro Ames rules, or the Gradient supplemental rules) sets 1.5 µg/day; otherwise a Gradient supplemental class, else the Revised Cramer class, sets 90, 540 or 1800 µg/day. The per-sniff inhaled mass is estimated from vapor pressure (CompTox or EPI Suite) and molecular weight assuming a 0.5 L average sniff (Laing 1983). The inhalation-exposure framing follows Mohar et al., Toxics 2025. The TTC is a generic threshold for chemicals without their own toxicity data and is generally conservative; where substance-specific data exist, they take precedence. This figure is a comparative reference — not a safety determination, exposure limit, or recommendation.

Intensity

Perceived intensity is predicted by the single-molecule network from the MixInt study (Pellegrino et al., bioRxiv 2025 — a preprint). A trained panel rated 62 odorants, each delivered as a known vapor-phase concentration from sealed sampling bags, and a neural network learned to map molecular features plus log concentration to the intensity rating. Ratings are on the generalized Labeled Magnitude Scale (gLMS; Green et al. 1996), whose verbal labels are spaced quasi-logarithmically: moderate sits at 17 and strong at 35 of 100. Its inputs include Dragon 6 3-D descriptors, vapor pressure and boiling point, which can't be computed in the browser, so the network is evaluated offline across a fine concentration grid for every molecule with those features. The page then reads the curve off that grid. Vapor pressures come from the MixInt dataset for the training odorants and from the EPA CompTox and EPI Suite for everything else (measured where available, then OPERA, then the MPBPVP estimate — the same method behind the training odorants' values). Predictions outside the rated concentration range, or for molecules unlike the 62 training odorants, are extrapolations.

Odor quality

Predicted descriptors come from OpenPOM, an open re-implementation of the Principal Odor Map message-passing neural network of Lee et al., Science 2023, which maps a molecular graph onto 138 odor-character labels. Results for 10,269 molecules are precomputed and looked up. For any other molecule the network itself — a message-passing GNN with a set2set readout, 2.1 million parameters — is downloaded and run in your browser, taking about 20 ms per molecule. Its outputs are uncalibrated — a score of 0.4 means something very different for a common label like sweet than for a rare one like vetiver — so each score is also shown as its rank among all molecules the model was run over. These are model predictions, not measurements, and are independent of the reported descriptors below.

For molecules present in public odor databases, the tool also lists the odor-character descriptors reported for them, aggregated via Pyrfume from Leffingwell, The Good Scents Company, Arctander (1960), IFRA (2019), Dravnieks (1985), Sigma-Aldrich and AromaDB. These are looked up per molecule, not predicted, so they appear only for molecules already characterized in those sources.
